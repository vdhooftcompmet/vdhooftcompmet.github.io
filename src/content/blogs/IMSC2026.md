IMSC 2026 kicked off on the weekend of 22–23 August, and for me it started with a slightly different role: stepping into the classroom as an instructor for the “Data Analysis & AI in MS” short course, together with Prof. Olga Vitek and Anthony Wu from Northeastern University.

Teaching a course at an international conference is a very different experience from simply attending one. Rather than presenting a finished piece of work, you are asked to create a space where people can interact, ask questions, challenge assumptions, and learn from each other. I particularly enjoyed how interactive the course became. The discussions went beyond the planned material, with participants bringing in their own experiences, challenges, and perspectives on data analysis and AI in mass spectrometry.

One of the things I appreciated most was that the exchange did not stop when we left the classroom. Many of the conversations continued throughout the conference, during poster sessions, coffee breaks, workshops, and informal discussions. For me, this was one of the most valuable aspects of the experience: teaching became a starting point for scientific exchange rather than a one-way transfer of knowledge.

The course was also a great opportunity to showcase some of the tools developed within our Computational Metabolomics group, including FERMO, SpecReBoot, MS2LDA2, and MS2Query. Being able to present these tools to a community working directly with mass spectrometry data was particularly valuable, as it allowed us to discuss not only what these approaches can do, but also how they can fit into real-world metabolomics workflows.

After the course, I had the opportunity to experience the rest of IMSC 2026 from the other side of the classroom, as a conference participant and researcher.

I was especially happy to bring my new tool, PhenoRewire, to the poster session. Presenting a new tool at this stage is not only about showing the final result. It is also about opening it up to the community, listening to how other researchers might use it, understanding where it could be improved, and gathering feedback that can shape its future development. The poster discussions were therefore an important opportunity to get fresh perspectives on PhenoRewire and to start conversations that may develop into new collaborations.

If you are interested in learning more about PhenoRewire, [here](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.29.748013v1) and the open-source repository [here](https://github.com/ldellavedova/PhenoRewire) are available online, where you can find more details about the approach and explore the tool yourself.

Overall, I really appreciated the balance of the scientific programme. The combination of plenary lectures, oral presentations, and dedicated time for poster sessions created different opportunities to engage with the science. The poster sessions in particular offered the chance to have more focused and detailed conversations, while the social activities and informal interactions provided the space to reconnect with colleagues, meet new people, and start conversations that would probably not happen in a more formal setting.

For me, this was one of the strongest aspects of IMSC 2026: there were opportunities not only to present and discuss science, but also to build the relationships around it.

I leave IMSC 2026 with new ideas, new conversations to follow up on, valuable feedback for the tools we are developing, and a renewed appreciation of how much scientific conferences are about people as much as presentations.

And now, back to the lab and the data.
